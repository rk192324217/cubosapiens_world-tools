## Introduction

GST touches nearly every purchase and invoice in India, but the mental model most people carry — four tax slabs of 5%, 12%, 18%, and 28% — is now outdated. India's GST system was significantly restructured under what's being called **GST 2.0**, effective September 22, 2025. This guide covers how GST works today, not the version most explainers online are still describing.

![Indian currency notes](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Banknote_of_india.png)

## What GST Actually Is

Goods and Services Tax is a destination-based, multi-stage indirect tax that replaced a patchwork of older taxes (VAT, service tax, excise duty, and others) when it launched in July 2017. "Multi-stage" means tax is collected at every step of a supply chain, but businesses can claim credit for tax already paid on their inputs — so the end consumer, not each business in the chain, effectively bears the final tax.

For domestic transactions within a state, GST splits into CGST (Central) and SGST (State), each charged at half the total rate. For transactions between states, IGST applies as a single combined rate.

## What Changed Under GST 2.0

The biggest structural change in GST's history: the previous four-slab system (5%, 12%, 18%, 28%) has been simplified down to essentially two core slabs, plus a special rate for a small category of goods.

- **0% (Nil)** — many essential items, including several food staples, life-saving medicines, and individual health and life insurance premiums, which are now fully exempt.
- **5%** — the lower merit rate, covering everyday essentials and mass-consumption goods that were often previously at 12%.
- **18%** — the standard rate, now covering most goods and services that don't qualify for the lower or nil rate.
- **40%** — a new "demerit" rate reserved for luxury and sin goods (premium cars, tobacco, aerated drinks, and similar categories), effectively replacing what used to be 28% plus a separate compensation cess.
- A few narrow legacy rates (like 3% for gold and silver) still exist outside this main structure.

The 12% and 28% general slabs have been removed. If you're pricing a product or filing returns using the old four-slab mental model, it's worth double-checking against the current rate for that specific category, since a lot of goods shifted brackets rather than just changing rate.

## How to Calculate GST

The math itself didn't change, only the applicable rates:

**Adding GST (GST-exclusive amount known):**
`Final Price = Base Price + (Base Price × GST Rate)`

For a ₹1,000 item at 18% GST: ₹1,000 + ₹180 = ₹1,180.

**Extracting GST (GST-inclusive amount known):**
`GST Amount = Inclusive Price × (Rate ÷ (100 + Rate))`

For a ₹1,180 invoice at 18% GST: ₹1,180 × (18 ÷ 118) = ₹180.

This reverse calculation trips people up most often — dividing the inclusive price by the rate directly (₹1,180 × 0.18) overstates the tax, because that 18% needs to be calculated against the pre-tax base, not the already-taxed total.

## Why This Matters for Freelancers and Small Businesses

If you invoice clients directly, using the wrong slab isn't just a rounding error — it can mean under-collecting tax you're still liable to remit, or overcharging a client relative to competitors using the correct rate. Since GST 2.0 reclassified many goods and services rather than applying a flat rate cut, it's worth re-checking your specific HSN/SAC code against the current rate list rather than assuming your old rate still applies.

## Closing Thought

GST 2.0 is explicitly designed to reduce classification disputes by collapsing the number of slabs — but that only helps once your invoicing, pricing, and calculations actually reflect the new structure. If you last checked your applicable rate before September 2025, it's worth a five-minute recheck.
